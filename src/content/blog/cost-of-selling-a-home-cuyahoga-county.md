When you sell a home, the number on the purchase agreement isn't the number that lands in your bank account. Between the sale price and your proceeds sit your mortgage payoff, county fees, a property tax credit to the buyer and a handful of smaller costs. None of them are hard to plan for once you know they're coming. Here's what sellers in Cuyahoga County typically pay, and where the local rules make a real difference.

## Start with your mortgage payoff

Your loan balance on a statement isn't quite the amount it takes to close it out. The payoff figure includes interest up to the closing date and any fees your lender charges. Your title company requests an official payoff statement before closing, but you can ask your lender for an estimate early so your net proceeds math is realistic from the start. If you have a home equity loan or line of credit, that gets paid off at closing too.

## Plan for the county conveyance fee

Ohio charges a conveyance fee whenever real estate changes hands, and the seller customarily pays it. In Cuyahoga County the fee is $4 for every $1,000 of the purchase price, plus a small transfer fee.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup> On a $250,000 sale, that's about $1,000. On a $400,000 sale, about $1,600. It's paid through your title company when the deed is recorded, so it shows up as a line on your settlement statement rather than a separate bill.

## Understand the property tax credit to the buyer

This is the cost that surprises sellers most. Ohio property taxes are paid in arrears, which means the bill you pay in a given half-year covers a period that already passed. When you sell, the taxes for the time you owned the home haven't all been billed yet, and the buyer will end up paying them. So at closing, the seller gives the buyer a credit for that share.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></sup>

Because Ohio property taxes are billed twice a year, a seller can owe several months to more than a year of taxes in that credit, depending on when you close.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></sup> The exact method is set by your purchase agreement, and different contract language can produce very different numbers. Read that section closely, and ask your agent or title company to estimate the credit before you accept an offer.

## Budget for point-of-sale inspections and repairs

Several Cuyahoga County suburbs require a city inspection before a home can be sold, and the city's list of violations can affect your closing.

- In **Cleveland Heights**, sellers need a point-of-sale certificate before signing a sales agreement, and money is held in escrow at closing for certain violations that aren't fixed before the transfer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">[4]</a></sup>
- In **Shaker Heights**, violations must be repaired or covered by an escrow of 150% of the estimated repair cost before the transfer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">[5]</a></sup>

Order the inspection as early as your city allows. Knowing the list before you list your home lets you choose which repairs to make, which to price in, and which a buyer may agree to take on.

## Agent commissions and concessions

Real estate commissions aren't set by law. They're negotiable, and since 2024 buyers sign a written agreement with their own agent that spells out how that agent is paid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-6">[6]</a></sup> In some sales the buyer asks the seller to contribute toward their agent's fee or their closing costs. Treat that like any other term in an offer: something to weigh against the price and the rest of the contract, not an automatic yes or no.

## Smaller costs to expect

- **Deed preparation and title charges.** Your title company and attorney fees for preparing the deed and handling the closing.
- **Prorated utilities and HOA dues.** Water and sewer bills are often settled at closing, and some communities charge a transfer or statement fee.
- **Pre-listing repairs and preparation.** Paint, minor fixes, cleaning and staging. These are optional, and some pay for themselves while others don't.
- **Moving costs.** Easy to forget when you're focused on the sale.

## Check whether you'll owe tax on your profit

Most homeowners selling their primary home don't owe federal tax on the profit. If you owned and lived in the home for at least two of the five years before the sale, you can generally exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 if you're married and file jointly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-7">[7]</a></sup> If you've rented the home out, owned it only briefly, or expect a gain above those limits, talk with a tax professional before you list.
