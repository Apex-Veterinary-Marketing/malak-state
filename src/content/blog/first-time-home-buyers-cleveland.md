Maybe you're renting in Ohio City and tired of paying someone else's mortgage. Maybe you've been scrolling listings in Lakewood, Parma or Shaker Heights and wondering what it would actually take to buy. Buying your first home in Cleveland comes with new vocabulary, new costs and a few local rules most guides skip. This one covers who qualifies as a first-time buyer, where to find down payment help in Cuyahoga County, and the steps from your first call to the day you get your keys.

## Find out if you count as a first-time home buyer

"First-time buyer" is a legal definition, not just a description, and it's broader than most people expect. The Ohio Housing Finance Agency (OHFA) treats you as a first-time buyer if you haven't had an ownership interest in your primary home at any point in the past three years. Qualified veterans with an honorable discharge count even if they've owned before, and in certain designated census tracts the first-time requirement doesn't apply at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup>

So if you owned a condo years ago, sold it and have rented since, you may still qualify for first-time buyer programs.

## Use down payment help available to Cleveland buyers

Most first-time buyers assume they need 20% down. Many loan programs ask for far less, and several public programs can help cover what's left.

### Ohio Housing Finance Agency programs

OHFA offers first mortgages for eligible buyers along with down payment assistance equal to a percentage of the purchase price, which can go toward the down payment or closing costs. The assistance is forgiven after a set number of years if you keep living in the home. Income limits, purchase price limits and minimum credit scores apply and vary by county, and you apply through an OHFA-participating lender.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></sup> Percentages and terms change from time to time, so ask your lender for the current numbers before you plan around them.

### Cuyahoga County Down Payment Assistance

The county's program offers up to 10% of the purchase price, capped at $20,900, to buyers with household incomes at or below 80% of the area median income who complete homeownership classes. You don't have to be a first-time buyer, but you can't have owned a home in the last three years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></sup>

Here's the local detail that matters: the program covers 51 communities in the county but **excludes the City of Cleveland, Cleveland Heights, East Cleveland, Euclid and Lakewood**, because of how its federal funding is defined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></sup> If you're open to several areas, that one fact can change where you look. Some of the excluded cities run their own programs, so it's worth asking about those too.

### Ohio Homebuyer Plus savings accounts

Not ready to buy yet? The Ohio Treasurer's Homebuyer Plus program lets Ohio residents open a savings account at a participating bank or credit union that pays an above-market interest rate, as long as the money goes toward buying a home in Ohio. Contributions may also lower your Ohio income tax.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">[4]</a></sup>

## Budget for more than the down payment

A realistic budget includes closing costs, a home inspection, moving costs and a cushion for repairs in your first year. Property taxes deserve a close look in Greater Cleveland, because they can vary a lot between neighboring cities and school districts.

Newer and fully renovated homes inside Cleveland often carry a city tax abatement. For single-family homes, it can cover 85% to 100% of the increase in assessed taxes from the new construction or renovation, depending on where the home is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-8">[8]</a></sup> Before you fall in love with an abated home, ask how many years remain and what the tax bill will be once it ends.

## Know the local rules that surprise first-time buyers

### Point-of-sale inspections in some suburbs

Several Cuyahoga County suburbs require a city inspection before a home can be sold. In Cleveland Heights, sellers must get a point-of-sale certificate before signing a sales agreement, and money is held in escrow for certain violations that aren't fixed before the transfer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">[5]</a></sup> Shaker Heights requires either repairs or an escrow of 150% of the estimated repair cost before the transfer, and a buyer who takes on unfinished repairs sets up the escrow account.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-6">[6]</a></sup> Read the city's report early.

### Older homes and lead paint

Much of Greater Cleveland's housing is decades old, which is part of its charm. For any home built before 1978, sellers must disclose known lead-based paint and hazards, and you get a 10-day window to have the home tested for lead unless you waive it in writing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-7">[7]</a></sup>

## Follow the steps from first call to closing

1. **Get pre-approved.** A lender reviews your income, credit and savings and tells you what you can borrow. This is also when you ask about OHFA and county programs, since you have to use a participating lender.
2. **Sign a buyer agreement.** Before touring homes, you'll sign a written agreement with your agent that explains how they're paid. Commissions are negotiable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-10">[10]</a></sup>
3. **Tour and make an offer.** Your agent pulls comparable sales so your offer is competitive and still protects you with the right contingencies.
4. **Inspect and appraise.** Your home inspection shows the home's condition, and the lender's appraisal confirms its value.
5. **Review your Closing Disclosure.** By law, you must receive it at least three business days before closing, so you have time to compare it with your Loan Estimate and ask questions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-9">[9]</a></sup>
6. **Close and get your keys.** Sign the final documents and the home is yours.

## Avoid the most common first-time buyer mistakes

- **Shopping before you're pre-approved.** It's hard to compete in a multiple-offer situation without a pre-approval letter.
- **Emptying your savings at closing.** Keep a reserve for the furnace, water heater or roof that will eventually need attention.
- **Opening new credit before closing.** A new car loan or credit card can change your loan approval. Wait until you have your keys.
- **Choosing by price alone.** Taxes, point-of-sale repairs and abatements can make a cheaper home cost more each month.
