Every Ohio home sale comes with paperwork, and one of the most important pieces is a few pages long and easy to skim past: the Residential Property Disclosure Form. For sellers, it's a legal obligation. For buyers, it's the first look at what the current owners know about the house. Here's what the form covers, who has to fill it out, and what happens if it arrives late.

## What the disclosure form is

Ohio law requires most sellers of residential property to complete a disclosure form written by the Ohio Department of Commerce and give it to the buyer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></sup> It's the same form statewide, and the current version is set by the Ohio Administrative Code.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></sup> The seller fills it out, not the agent, and signs it.

The form covers the parts of a home where problems are expensive and hard to spot on a showing, including:

- The water supply and sewer or septic system
- The roof, and any leaks or water in the basement or crawl space
- Structural problems with the foundation, walls or floors
- Wood-destroying insects
- Mechanical systems like electrical, plumbing, heating and air conditioning
- Environmental hazards such as radon, asbestos, lead-based paint and underground storage tanks
- Whether the home is in a flood plain or a Lake Erie coastal erosion area
- Known building or zoning code violations, boundary disputes and shared driveways

That Lake Erie question matters more here than almost anywhere else in the state. If you're buying near the shoreline in Lakewood, Bay Village, Euclid or another lakefront community, read that answer carefully.

## What sellers are, and aren't, responsible for

Sellers answer based on their **actual knowledge**. You aren't expected to open up walls or hire an inspector to fill out the form, and you aren't liable for problems you didn't know about.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup> But anything you do know about belongs on the form, even if you think it was fixed. A wet basement that was repaired five years ago is worth disclosing along with the repair.

If you learn something new after giving the form to the buyer, such as a leak that shows up during the listing, talk with your agent about amending it. An honest, complete form protects you far more than a short one.

## Which sales don't need the form

Some transfers are exempt. They include court-ordered sales (including many sales during the administration of an estate), foreclosures and deeds in lieu of foreclosure, transfers by a fiduciary such as a trustee or estate administrator, transfers between co-owners, transfers to a spouse or a direct relative such as a child or parent, transfers to or from a government agency, and newly built homes that no one has lived in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup>

If you're buying a home in one of these situations, plan on doing more of your own homework, because you may not receive a disclosure form at all.

## Timing and the buyer's right to cancel

The form is supposed to reach the buyer **before** they sign the purchase agreement. If it arrives after, or if the seller amends it after the contract is signed, the buyer can cancel the agreement by delivering a signed, written notice within three business days of receiving the form. That right ends at whichever comes first: closing, or 30 days after the seller accepted the offer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></sup>

For sellers, this is the simplest reason to complete the form before you list. Having it ready means buyers see it up front, and nobody gets a surprise exit window after the deal is signed.

## The form doesn't replace an inspection

The form itself says it isn't a warranty and isn't a substitute for an inspection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></sup> A seller can only disclose what they know, and plenty of issues go unnoticed by people who live with them every day. Buyers should still get a professional home inspection, and consider radon, sewer line or other specialized inspections when the house calls for them.

## Other disclosures in Northeast Ohio

The state form isn't the only disclosure you'll see.

- **Lead-based paint.** For homes built before 1978, which describes much of Northeast Ohio, federal law requires a separate lead disclosure, and buyers get a 10-day window to test for lead unless they waive it in writing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **Point-of-sale reports.** Suburbs like Cleveland Heights require a city inspection before a sale, and the resulting report lists violations the buyer or seller will need to address.<sup class="cite"><a href="#source-5">[5]</a></sup>
